> **Research snapshot, October 2026**, behind the Android app's Router decisions.
> **Update after this report:** the `USAGE_ALARM` route was tested on the Nothing Phone (4a) Pro (Android 16):
> one alarm-usage stream was reported on `deviceIds:[speaker, bt_a2dp]` and routed to `[speaker, A2DP: CMF Buds Pro 2]`
> through a duplicated output. As predicted, both get the same signal, so they can't be time-aligned.

# Route around Android's one-headset Bluetooth wall

Without root, an Android 16 app cannot play to two classic A2DP headphones from the phone's own radio. AOSP allows **up to 5 A2DP sinks to be connected but only one to be active**, and the Bluetooth service, the native stack and audio policy each enforce that single active sink. No public or system API selects two. Three practical workarounds remain for the user's four classic headphones. The first is a **USB-C dual-link Bluetooth transmitter** (roughly 150–200 ms SBC latency, no per-headset delay control). The second is the **relay AudioPatch already has** (phone → PC → many outputs), which today costs about 300 ms of jitter buffer before the PC's own Bluetooth delay. The third is a **hybrid of the phone's Bluetooth for headset 1 and a USB-C dongle or DAC for headset 2**, which is plausible but unproven. For speaker + Bluetooth, the only unprivileged mechanism in AOSP that genuinely duplicates output is **USAGE_ALARM**: its strategy routes to the speaker *plus* the media device, but both get one buffer, so the skew cannot be corrected. An **AAudio MMAP stream with setDeviceId** follows a different explicit-routing rule and is the most promising untested lever. LE Audio "Audio sharing" (Auracast) is the native multi-headset answer on Android 16, but none of these headphones can join it, and third-party apps cannot start it anyway. AudioPatch should therefore (1) cut the relay's latency by moving from RFCOMM to Wi-Fi UDP with Snapcast-style clock sync and per-output delay alignment, (2) support and test the USB-C hybrid, and (3) ship USAGE_ALARM speaker duplication as an experimental mode only after on-device tests. The ranked plan and test list are at the end.

## Three layers of Android hard-code a single active A2DP sink

The Nothing Phone (4a) Pro behaves exactly like stock AOSP. In `A2dpService`, a single guarded field, `mActiveDevice`, holds the active device, and `setActiveDevice()` overwrites it. The "Maximum connected Bluetooth audio devices" cap (`mMaxConnectedAudioDevices`) only limits how many sinks may be *connected or connecting* ([A2dpService.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/packages/modules/Bluetooth/+/refs/heads/main/android/app/src/com/android/bluetooth/a2dp/A2dpService.java)). The native layer goes further: `btif_av.cc` stores one `active_peer_`, and `SetActivePeer()` **tears down the old peer's session before starting the new one**. It switches the stream; it does not fan it out ([btif_av.cc](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/packages/modules/Bluetooth/+/refs/heads/main/system/btif/src/btif_av.cc)). The audio framework registers A2DP with audio policy only through the active-device path (`makeA2dpDeviceAvailable(..., "onSetBtActiveDevice")`), keeping "only one by device type" ([AudioDeviceInventory.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/frameworks/base/+/refs/heads/main/services/core/java/com/android/server/audio/AudioDeviceInventory.java)). As a result, **a second connected headset never even appears as a routable `AudioDeviceInfo`**, so `setPreferredDevice` has nothing to target.

The developer option only writes `persist.bluetooth.maxconnectedaudiodevices`, which is clamped to [1, 5] with an AOSP default of 5. The value is read once at service start ([AdapterProperties.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/packages/modules/Bluetooth/+/refs/heads/main/android/app/src/com/android/bluetooth/btservice/AdapterProperties.java); [Settings controller](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/packages/apps/Settings/+/refs/heads/main/src/com/android/settings/development/BluetoothMaxConnectedAudioDevicesPreferenceController.java)). Users have reported this exact symptom for years: both headsets listed, "only one can be active at a time" ([Fairphone forum](https://forum.fairphone.com/t/bluetooth-audio-streaming-to-two-devices/79885)). The hidden `BluetoothA2dp.setActiveDevice()` is `@hide`. On the service side it checks only BLUETOOTH_CONNECT, so reflection or Shizuku could reach it, but it still only *switches* devices ([BluetoothA2dp.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/packages/modules/Bluetooth/+/refs/heads/main/framework/java/android/bluetooth/BluetoothA2dp.java)). The other escape routes are also closed. An app can open RFCOMM/L2CAP sockets, but headphone firmware accepts audio only over AVDTP, whose channel the system stack owns. ASHA needs a hearing-aid GATT service in the sink's firmware and models a binaural left/right pair as one sink ([AOSP ASHA spec](https://source.android.com/docs/core/connect/bluetooth/asha)).

Samsung's Dual Audio shows that a vendor *can* modify its stack to do this. Even there, Samsung "doesn't have any calibration tools" for the audible offset between the two outputs ([Android Central](https://www.androidcentral.com/samsung-dual-audio)). It does not exist on Nothing OS. Play Store "Bluetooth splitter" apps cannot get around these layers on a stock phone, so they rely on OEM features, use a Wi-Fi join-from-your-phone design, or are misleading. That conclusion follows from the code; no specific listing was checked.

## Audio policy routes per strategy, which explains the speaker override

The measured failure, where `AudioTrack.setPreferredDevice(speaker)` was overridden to the active A2DP headset, is designed behaviour. The default engine honours explicit routing "only if all active clients have a preferred route" ([Engine.cpp](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_av/blob/lineage-23.0/services/audiopolicy/enginedefault/src/Engine.cpp)). Per output, `findPreferredDevice` returns null as soon as an active client from another UID has no route ([AudioIODescriptorInterface.h](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_av/blob/lineage-23.0/services/audiopolicy/common/managerdefinitions/include/AudioIODescriptorInterface.h)). The policy manager states the intent plainly: "a specific app can not force routing for other apps by setting a preferred device" ([AudioPolicyManager.cpp](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_av/blob/lineage-23.0/services/audiopolicy/managerdefault/AudioPolicyManager.cpp)). For AudioPatch, the **app being captured is itself an unrouted USAGE_MEDIA client**, so it pulls the whole MEDIA strategy back to its default device, A2DP. Even with no other app playing, two of AudioPatch's own MEDIA tracks would collapse onto one device. Google's position on AAudio is the same: "setDeviceId() does not guarantee that an app will get the requested device. DSP limitations may force device sharing," and the limit sits in AudioPolicy and the DSP, so "all APIs would probably be the same" ([Oboe #1472](https://github.com/google/oboe/issues/1472)).

Two narrow doors remain. The first is **STRATEGY_SONIFICATION**, which covers USAGE_ALARM and USAGE_NOTIFICATION_TELEPHONY_RINGTONE. Its routing selects SPEAKER (or SPEAKER_SAFE) and then falls through to add "the same device used by media strategy", i.e. the last connected A2DP/wired/USB device ([Engine.cpp](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_av/blob/lineage-23.0/services/audiopolicy/enginedefault/src/Engine.cpp)). When the second device lives on another HAL module, the policy manager opens a **duplicating output thread** against the primary output ([AudioPolicyManager.cpp](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_av/blob/lineage-23.0/services/audiopolicy/managerdefault/AudioPolicyManager.cpp)). Google's own AudioTrack documentation cites "an alarm playing on headphones and speaker on a phone" as the case where `getRoutedDevices()` returns several devices ([AudioTrack.java](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_base/blob/lineage-23.0/media/java/android/media/AudioTrack.java)). USAGE_ASSISTANCE_SONIFICATION does *not* work this way, because it maps to STRATEGY_MEDIA ([EngineDefaultConfig.h](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_av/blob/lineage-23.0/services/audiopolicy/engine/common/src/EngineDefaultConfig.h)). Alarm duplication has costs:

- **Volume:** the stream follows the ALARM volume.
- **Calls:** it is remapped to the phone strategy during calls.
- **Other outputs:** an active alarm can reroute other outputs on the same module.
- **Skew:** both devices get one mixed buffer, so the speaker leads the Bluetooth path by the full A2DP pipeline delay and the app cannot delay just one leg.
- **Doubled audio:** the source app keeps playing on A2DP, so the headset hears the original *and* AudioPatch's alarm copy, offset by capture latency. Unless the original can be silenced, the headset leg produces an echo.

That last problem was not covered by any source, and it decides whether this mode is usable at all.

The second door is **AAudio MMAP**. Its rule is different: on MMAP I/Os "the preferred device is selected by the first client (virtual client created when the mmap stream is opened)", and the active state of other clients is not checked ([AudioIODescriptorInterface.h](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_av/blob/lineage-23.0/services/audiopolicy/common/managerdefinitions/include/AudioIODescriptorInterface.h)). An exclusive, low-latency Oboe stream with `setDeviceId(speaker)` could therefore keep a speaker path while MEDIA stays on A2DP. This is a hypothesis, not a confirmed result. It fails if the phone's MMAP mix port does not reach the speaker, or if A2DP offload puts both devices on one DSP that refuses the combination. Oboe #1472 also records the opposite side effect on Samsung and Pixel: a speaker-routed stream dragged other audio onto the speaker ([Oboe #1472](https://github.com/google/oboe/issues/1472)). Oboe's OpenSL ES path ignores `setDeviceId` entirely ([Oboe #228](https://github.com/google/oboe/issues/228)). The privileged levers are out of reach for a sideloaded app: `registerAudioPolicy`, `setPreferredDeviceForStrategy`, `setForceUse` and the LE broadcast APIs all require MODIFY_AUDIO_ROUTING or BLUETOOTH_PRIVILEGED, which are signature|privileged permissions ([AndroidManifest.xml](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_base/blob/lineage-23.0/core/res/AndroidManifest.xml); [AudioManager.java](https://github.com/LineageOS/android_frameworks_base/blob/lineage-23.0/media/java/android/media/AudioManager.java)).

## USB-C transmitters are the only phone-only fix for two classic headsets

Because USB audio is a separate HAL module and output, hardware can do what the phone's radio will not. Products advertised as dual-link that plug into a USB-C phone include the **Avantree C81** ("pair your headphones normally… then pair the next set" ([Headphonesty](https://www.headphonesty.com/2022/09/connect-two-bluetooth-headphones-android/))), the **Twelve South AirFly USB-C** with dual pairing ([B&H](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/1526996-REG/twelve_south_12_1912_airfly_usb_c_wireless_headphone.html)), and the **KOKKIA USB splitter**, which auto-pairs headset 1 and pairs headset 2 at a button press ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/KOKKIA-USB_Splitter-Transmitter-Smartphones-wirelessly/dp/B07F242D38)). The PC-oriented **Avantree DG80P** (US$29.99, out of stock, no Android listed) ([Avantalk](https://www.avantalk.com/products/usb-bluetooth-audio-transmitter-for-pc-dg80p)) and some generic dongles that "can NOT pair with any mobile phone" ([Newegg](https://www.newegg.com/p/2S7-08DZ-003H1)) show that USB Audio Class compatibility with phones has to be checked per product. Avantree's advice is to confirm first that the phone's USB-C port outputs audio, and it notes that only the first headset's microphone works ([Avantree](https://avantree.com/blogs/news/how-to-connect-two-headphones-to-one-android-phone-heres-what-you-need)).

Latency is limited by the headphones, not by the dongle. The CMF Buds Pro 2 support SBC, AAC and LDAC but **no aptX** ([Nothing support](https://support.nothing.tech/hc/en-us/articles/26468752574481-What-audio-formats-does-CMF-Buds-Pro-2-support)). The JBL and ZEB spec pages name no aptX variant ([JBL Tune 770NC](https://support.jbl.com/ca/en/headphones/headphones-bluetooth-wireless/TUNE770NC.html); [Zebronics](https://zebronics.com/products/zeb-paradise-neo-r)). The "40 ms aptX LL" figures on transmitter boxes therefore do not apply, and the realistic range is Avantree's **SBC ≈150–200 ms** ([Avantree](https://avantree.com/blogs/knowledge/how-to-fix-echo-problems-in-a-headset-or-headphones)) or 1Mii's **70–200 ms for non-LL receivers** ([desertcart/1Mii](https://www.desertcart.sg/products/226391825)). Sync between two *different* models is uncorrected, because no product found offers a per-sink delay. Avantree also documents dual-link dropouts caused by interoperability and bandwidth ([Avantree KB](https://avantree.com/knowledge-base/poor-audio/)). Retail reviews of dual-link units range from "works fine with two AirPods" to "a 2 s delay between the two" ([desertcart reviews](https://www.desertcart.ma/products/55725617)).

The more interesting configuration for AudioPatch is the **hybrid**: the phone's own Bluetooth keeps headset 1 (on AAC/LDAC), and a cheap single-sink USB-C dongle or a USB-C DAC drives headset 2 or wired speakers. A2DP and USB sit in different HAL modules, so both outputs can be open at once ([AOSP audio accessories](https://source.android.com/devices/accessories/audio?hl=en)). The per-strategy rule still applies, though. When the dongle is plugged in, default MEDIA follows the most recently connected removable device, and the source app's unrouted MEDIA track goes with it. AudioPatch's copy then has to reach A2DP by some route that escapes MEDIA. No source confirms or rules this out, so it needs testing on the device. If it works, it is the one hardware-assisted path where **the app controls both legs and can delay the faster one** to align the two headsets.

## The relay already works, but RFCOMM jitter costs most of the latency budget

Off-phone relays are the only software path to *any number* of classic headphones. Their latency comes almost entirely from buffering, not the network. On healthy Wi-Fi, AudioRelay measures **about 10 ms network latency**, considers 30–50 ms buffers sustainable, and calls 10 ms "probably too low" ([AudioRelay FAQ](https://audiorelay.net/docs/faq)). In practice its users see 100–120 ms floors ([community](https://community.audiorelay.net/t/max-buffer-wont-go-lower-than-120ms/359)) and about 500 ms when chaining PC → phone → Bluetooth hearing aids ([community](https://community.audiorelay.net/t/audio-delay-when-relaying-pc-sound-from-mobile-to-hearing-aids/1344)). AudioPatch's measured RFCOMM gaps of ~150 ms force a ~300 ms jitter buffer, which is roughly 6–10× what a Wi-Fi link needs. On the PC, a Bluetooth headset then adds its own 150–280 ms (Avantree puts classic SBC at 180–280 ms ([Avantree](https://support.avantree.com/hc/en-us/articles/54932119946521-Why-do-I-notice-audio-delay-with-Bluetooth-Auracast))). End to end, the result is probably **around 450–600 ms**. That is fine for music and unusable for video.

Snapcast shows how to sync multiple receivers. Clients estimate the clock offset from timestamped round trips, and the offset is (c2s − s2c)/2 ([Snapcast protocol](https://github.com/badaix/snapcast/blob/develop/doc/binary_protocol.md)). Each chunk carries a server-time play-at stamp, and clients drop or duplicate single samples, which keeps **deviation typically below 0.2 ms**. Opus is listed as the low-latency codec, and running it at 48 kHz avoids resampling delay ([Snapcast README](https://github.com/badaix/snapcast)). That accuracy holds only up to each DAC, so the PC fan-out must also subtract each output's own latency. With Bluetooth outputs this is largely guesswork. Classic A2DP has no negotiated render-delay value comparable to ASHA's `RenderDelay` field ([AOSP ASHA spec](https://source.android.com/docs/core/connect/bluetooth/asha)), so a per-output manual offset slider (or a mic-based calibration) is required. On the transport side, one developer reports **steady 20 ms delivery of L2CAP CoC packets between two Android phones** ([Nordic DevZone](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/129090/ble-l2cap-coc-reception-delay---nrf54l15-android-to-nrf-20ms-sent-100-200ms-received/570891)). That makes CoC worth measuring against RFCOMM when Wi-Fi is not available, though the evidence is thin.

Lip-sync sets the target. ITU-R BT.1359 puts the detectability limit at about **125 ms of audio lag and the acceptability limit at about 185 ms** ([ITU-R BT.1359](https://itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bt/R-REC-BT.1359-0-199802-S!!PDF-E.pdf)). Android video players compensate for the device they play to: Bluetooth sinks report 200–300 ms through AVDTP delay reports, and ExoPlayer and MediaPlayer apps adjust for it ([VLC-Android #1487](https://code.videolan.org/videolan/vlc-android/-/issues/1487)). AudioPlaybackCapture sits *downstream* of the player, so the relay's extra delay is invisible to it. A design consequence follows (an inference, not a documented technique): if the phone itself plays to a slow route that the player compensates for, AudioPatch can schedule receivers against the phone's *actual playout time* rather than capture time, and receivers within ~200–300 ms of capture would then line up with the video. The existing **phone-to-PC A2DP via AudioPlaybackConnection** is relevant here. In that mode the PC *is* the phone's A2DP sink, so the phone's player compensates for whatever delay Windows reports. Measuring that reported delay could make it the best video mode AudioPatch already has.

## Auracast fixes multi-headphone playback, but not for these headphones

Android 16 QPR1 brought native **Audio sharing** to Pixel 8 and newer, after Samsung (One UI 7) and Sony Xperia models. It sends one stream to several LE Audio headsets "in sync", and both phone and headsets must support LE Audio with Auracast ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2025/09/03/auracast-le-audio-sharing-coming-to-pixel/); [Google blog](https://blog.google/products/android/le-audio-auracast-support/)). Vendor figures put Auracast at 40–60 ms ([Avantree](https://support.avantree.com/hc/en-us/articles/54932119946521-Why-do-I-notice-audio-delay-with-Bluetooth-Auracast)). The Nothing Phone (4a) Pro reports broadcast-source support, but a third-party app cannot use it. `BluetoothLeBroadcast` is `@SystemApi`, and `startBroadcast` requires BLUETOOTH_PRIVILEGED ([AOSP BluetoothLeBroadcast.java](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/packages/modules/Bluetooth/+/refs/heads/main/framework/java/android/bluetooth/BluetoothLeBroadcast.java)). The most an app can do is open Bluetooth settings so the user can go to Connection preferences > Audio sharing ([Pixel Help](https://support.google.com/pixelphone/answer/16483797)). No direct deep-link intent was found, and whether Nothing OS shows the sharing UI at all is unverified.

None of the four headphones can join a broadcast today. The one with a future is the **JBL Tune 770NC**, marketed with "LE Audio*… available via OTA update at a later stage" ([JBL](https://global.jbl.com/over-ear-headphones/TUNE770NC.html)). JBL has delivered this for other models before (the Tour One M2 got Auracast in firmware 5.6.0) ([JBL release notes](https://support.jbl.com/ca/en/howto/tour-one-m2-software-update-release-notes-us/000047991.html)), but no 770NC release with it was found, and "LE Audio" does not guarantee broadcast-sink support. Auracast-to-classic bridges such as Avantree's AuraLink output to AUX/RCA ([Avantree AuraLink](https://avantree.com/products/auralink-bluetooth-auracast-receiver)). A broadcast-to-A2DP re-transmit would add a second Bluetooth hop, which is worse than a dual-link USB-C dongle.

## Ranked recommendation for AudioPatch

**1. Make the relay the flagship, and replace RFCOMM jitter with Wi-Fi.** This is the only path that serves two-plus classic headphones with no extra hardware, and AudioPatch already owns it. Add a UDP transport over the LAN or the phone's hotspot, with 10–20 ms Opus frames at 48 kHz, Snapcast-style offset estimation (take the minimum RTT over many samples), server-time play-at stamps, and sample drop/duplicate drift correction. Keep RFCOMM as the fallback, and benchmark L2CAP CoC with the same harness. On the PC, add a per-output delay offset so a JBL over-ear and CMF earbuds line up. Send timestamped silence instead of pausing, since AudioRelay users report latency jumps after silence ([community](https://community.audiorelay.net/t/big-latency-after-silence/1053)). The target is a buffer under 80 ms, which brings wired PC outputs inside the lip-sync window.

**2. Support the USB-C hybrid and the dual-link dongle as the "no PC" answer.** Detect `TYPE_USB_DEVICE`/`TYPE_USB_HEADSET` outputs and tell the user what the dongle can and cannot do (SBC-class latency, first headset's mic only). If the hybrid test below passes, route AudioPatch's copy to the second leg and expose a delay slider.

**3. Ship speaker + Bluetooth via USAGE_ALARM only as a labelled experiment**, and only if tests show the doubled-audio problem on the headset leg can be avoided. **4. Prototype AAudio MMAP speaker routing**; if it holds, it is a better speaker + Bluetooth mode than the alarm route, because the app controls each leg's delay. **5. For Auracast**, provide a settings shortcut and a note that LE Audio headphones (possibly a future JBL 770NC firmware) unlock native sharing. Do not build on reflection `setActiveDevice` (it is a switcher, not a splitter) or on raw sockets to headphones.

## Conclusion

The real constraint is not Bluetooth bandwidth or radio hardware. Android deliberately routes per *strategy* and ignores per-*track* wishes whenever another app is playing, and a capture-and-replay app is by definition always running beside another app. That makes AudioPatch's architecture an awkward fit for on-phone routing and a good fit for off-phone fan-out. The two on-phone exceptions, alarm duplication and MMAP's first-client rule, are side doors in the policy rather than features, and either could be closed on Nothing's vendor engine. Only device tests will show whether they are open.

The relay's weakness is mostly self-inflicted and therefore fixable. Network delay on Wi-Fi is about 10 ms, and the 300 ms RFCOMM jitter buffer accounts for most of the delay. Bluetooth's inherent delay on each receiver cannot be removed, but it can be measured and aligned. A clock-synced Wi-Fi relay with per-output offsets would give AudioPatch something no dongle or OEM Dual Audio offers: several classic headphones of different models, delay-matched to each other, with a credible route to acceptable lip-sync.
